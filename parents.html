Why it matters

How Sports Help Children Grow and Build Resilience

Sports can help children feel safe, regulate their emotions, and build resilience when three elements work together: trusted relationships, varied movement and predictable routines, and challenges that match the child's readiness.

Relationships and trust

Children are more willing to try difficult things and recover from mistakes when they trust their coaches and feel that they belong with their teammates. Attention, dignity, and encouragement should not depend on ability, playing time, or the result of the last play.

Movement and predictable routines

A familiar welcome, warm-up, transition, reset routine, and closing reflection help athletes know what to expect and direct their attention toward learning. Good routines provide stability without eliminating creativity, athlete choice, or free play.

Manageable stress and age-appropriate challenge

Healthy sports do not remove difficulty. Coaches should teach the underlying skill, create an attainable first step, add complexity gradually, and give athletes another opportunity to try. The goal is productive challenge rather than boredom on one side or overwhelming fear on the other.

Questions worth asking

  • Does the coach know the child as a person and understand why they play?
  • Can athletes make mistakes, ask questions, and raise concerns without humiliation?
  • Does every child receive instruction and connection, not only the strongest performers?
  • What can athletes count on every time they arrive?
  • Does challenge increase as skills and confidence develop?
  • After a mistake, does the child receive a useful next step and another chance?

For families

1 Help Children Stay Connected

A child wanting to step away is information. It is not automatically a failure of commitment. The first job is to understand whether the issue is temporary frustration, the specific environment, physical or emotional overload, or a genuine change in interest.

What to notice

  • A noticeable loss of excitement before practices or games
  • Recurring physical complaints or unusual fatigue
  • Fear of mistakes, coaches, teammates, or disappointing adults
  • A schedule crowding out sleep, friendships, school, or family life
  • The child no longer feeling included, useful, or able to improve

Questions to ask your child

  • What is still fun about this sport?
  • Do you feel watched or evaluated every time you practice or compete?
  • What would you change if you could?
  • Do you want a break, a different team, a different role, or a different activity?
  • Do you feel safe speaking honestly with your coach?
  • Would you choose this schedule if nobody else expected it?

What you can do

  • Listen before persuading or solving.
  • Separate the child's experience from the family's investment of time and money.
  • Consider changing the environment before deciding the child no longer likes the sport.
  • Protect at least some unstructured time and friendships outside the team.

For families

2 Evaluate the Environment

A strong program should be able to explain how it develops children, trains coaches, protects safety, communicates with families, and responds when concerns arise. Winning and healthy development are not opposites, but winning should never excuse unsafe or demeaning behavior.

What to notice

  • Coaches teach and communicate in ways appropriate for the athletes' ages.
  • Mistakes are treated as part of learning rather than as personal failures.
  • Children receive meaningful instruction, attention, and opportunities to contribute.
  • Safety policies and reporting paths are clear.
  • The culture respects opponents, officials, teammates, and families.

Questions to ask the program

  • How are coaches selected, screened, and trained?
  • How does the program define development and success at this age?
  • How are playing time, positions, and roster decisions handled?
  • How can athletes share concerns or feedback?
  • What is the process if a parent has a health, safety, or conduct concern?

What you can do

  • Review the Children's Bill of Rights before joining a program.
  • Ask questions before making a financial or time commitment.
  • Address concerns calmly and through the organization's stated process.
  • Model the behavior you want from coaches and athletes.

For families

3 Choose a Development Path

The best development plan is not necessarily the busiest or most expensive one. It should match the child's maturity, motivation, health, and family circumstances while leaving room for recovery, school, friendships, and life outside sports.

What to notice

  • The child, not only the parent, wants the added commitment.
  • Coaching quality and practice design matter more than branding or tournament volume.
  • The schedule allows adequate sleep, recovery, and time away from organized training.
  • The opportunity teaches skills or creates experiences the child cannot reasonably obtain elsewhere.
  • The family understands the total cost, travel, and calendar commitment before joining.

Questions worth asking

  • How many different ways does the child move during the year?
  • Does the child have a genuine off-season from sport-specific training and competition?
  • What specific developmental need does this opportunity meet?
  • Is the child asking for more, or are the adults afraid of falling behind?
  • Could the same benefit be found locally or through a shorter commitment?
  • What will the child give up to participate?
  • Can the family reevaluate the decision without shame if the fit changes?

What you can do

  • Build movement diversity through free play, biking, swimming, hiking, skating, playground activity, and other informal movement, not only additional formal teams.
  • Favor quality instruction and deliberate practice over sheer volume.
  • Encourage sport sampling and broad movement skills, especially for younger children.
  • Preserve free play and true time off.
  • Reassess the plan each season rather than assuming every commitment must automatically continue.
Movement diversityBroad movement development does not require enrollment in multiple formal teams. The useful question is not only how many sports a child plays, but how many different ways the child moves and whether the schedule includes real time away from the primary sport.

For families

4 Support Without Adding Pressure

Parents do not need to remove competition, disappointment, or failure from sports. Children need room to experience those things without also managing an adult's anxiety, disappointment, or technical evaluation.

Before practice or competition

  • Ask what kind of support the child wants from you.
  • Say, "I love watching you play. You do not need to impress me."
  • Avoid last-minute technical reminders and let the coach provide competitive instructions.
  • Give the child permission to stay with the next play instead of looking into the stands for approval.

At practice

There is no universal rule about watching practice. Ask whether your child enjoys having you there, follow supervision policies, and consider whether your presence helps the child experiment freely or makes every repetition feel evaluated.

  • Am I present because my child wants support, because supervision is required, or because I find it difficult not to monitor progress?
  • Can the coach and athlete build a relationship that does not run through me?

During competition

Practice is for correction. Game time is for play. Encourage effort, teamwork, and recovery. Let coaches coach, and be aware of what your face and body language communicate after a mistake.

  • When a child looks toward the stands, offer a steady nod or smile that says, "You are okay. Get the next one."
  • Cheer for the team and avoid competing instructions, visible frustration, and criticism of officials or teammates.

The ride home

Reconnect before evaluating. Offer water, food, music, quiet, or ordinary conversation. Let the child decide whether to discuss the game, and ask permission before beginning a performance conversation.

Questions that invite conversation

  • Did you have fun?
  • What was your favorite part?
  • What did you learn or find challenging?
  • Was there a moment when you helped a teammate?
  • Do you want to talk about it or just relax?

What to avoid

  • An immediate statistical or technical review
  • Replaying every mistake or criticizing the coach, officials, or teammates
  • Comparing the child with another athlete
  • Forcing a conversation while emotions are still high

Coach tool · A message to families

"Our athletes care deeply about the reactions of the adults watching them. During competition, please encourage effort, teamwork, and the next play while allowing the coaches to handle technical instruction. Mistakes are part of learning. Afterward, let your child lead the conversation. Our shared goal is a team environment in which every athlete can compete hard without fearing that one mistake will disappoint the adults who support them."

For families

5 Keep Recruiting in Perspective

About 2% of high-school athletes earn an athletic scholarship. That is different from the percentage who participate in college sports, which varies substantially by sport and division.

Families should keep the definition of success broad enough to include education, friendships, health, resilience, leadership, and a lasting relationship with physical activity.

What to notice

  • Coaches evaluate character, communication, academics, work habits, and coachability in addition to talent.
  • The athlete should gradually own communication, preparation, and decision-making.
  • The highest competitive level is not automatically the best educational or personal fit.
  • Spending more during childhood does not create a guaranteed scholarship return.
  • A healthy sports experience remains valuable even when recruiting never becomes part of the story.

Questions worth asking

  • Does the athlete genuinely want to pursue college sports?
  • Is the school a good academic, financial, social, and athletic fit?
  • What role and development opportunity is realistically available?
  • Is the athlete communicating directly and appropriately with coaches?
  • Would this still be a good college choice if sports ended unexpectedly?

What you can do

  • Keep academics and personal development central.
  • Let the athlete lead as maturity allows.
  • Seek honest evaluation from trusted coaches.
  • Compare the full cost of attendance, not merely the scholarship headline.
  • Celebrate the person and the process, not only the roster outcome.
College sports beyond Division IDivision II, Division III, NAIA, junior-college, and other collegiate opportunities can offer meaningful competition, education, friendships, leadership, and a strong campus community. The highest label is not automatically the best academic, financial, athletic, or personal fit.
Current rules and roster questionsEligibility rules, roster limits, scholarships, transfer policies, and recruiting practices change. Verify current information through the NCAA, NAIA, the institution, and the sport's governing resources rather than relying on an older guide, a showcase promise, or general advice that may not apply to the athlete's sport and graduation year.

Questions Families Are Asking

Questions submitted before the event reveal a consistent concern: families want to support ambition without sacrificing health, joy, family life, or realistic decision-making.

Will my child fall behind?

Development is not a race at age ten or twelve. Quality instruction, deliberate practice, broad movement, recovery, and sustained motivation matter more than accumulating the largest possible number of games and tournaments. Reassess the fit each season rather than treating every childhood decision as permanent.

When should an athlete specialize?

There is no universal age that fits every child and sport. Consider physical and emotional maturity, the child's own motivation, training volume, injury history, access to varied movement, and genuine time off. Intensive training can create risk even when a child technically participates in more than one sport.

What helps athletes continue developing?

No single personality trait guarantees long-term success. Coaches and families can protect athlete ownership, adaptability, trusted relationships, recovery, identity beyond sport, and the ability to respond to failure without shame.

How should travel programs set the schedule?

Programs should be able to explain what each tournament or competition adds to development. More games are not automatically better. Protect practice time, recovery, family capacity, and periods away from sport-specific training, then communicate those boundaries confidently to families.

How do athletes get recruited?

Recruiting differs by sport, level, institution, and roster need. Athletes should seek an honest evaluation, communicate directly as maturity allows, keep academics central, and use current governing-body resources. No travel team, showcase, or private service can guarantee recruitment or a future roster place.

What You Can Do Next

As a parent or caregiver

  • Ask your child what they enjoy most and what they would change.
  • Review the Children's Bill of Rights together.
  • Evaluate whether the current schedule fits your child's age, health, and interests.
  • Learn about local options before assuming your child must leave the community.
  • Endorse the Children's Bill of Rights and share it with your child's program.
